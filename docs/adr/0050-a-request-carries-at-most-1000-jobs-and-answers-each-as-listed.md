# A request carries at most 1,000 jobs and answers each as the job list gives it

A Batch sent in groups of jobs can stop between them, from a lost connection or a refusal in a later group, and then exists with part of its jobs, already running them. `POST /batches` and `POST /batches/{id}/jobs` each take up to 1,000 jobs in a body of at most 16 MiB, and commit them together (ADR 0049), so a Batch within both limits is created whole or not at all, through the API and through `batches submit`, which packs jobs into requests by both. A request over either limit is refused and makes nothing: more jobs with 400 pointing at `/jobs`, more bytes with 413. A larger Batch is sent in several requests, each committed on its own.

Both answer each job the request names as `GET /jobs` lists it, beside whether the request created it and the Batch: without its Output, its failure's message or its activity, which a read of the job gives. A request repeated after its jobs ended names every one of them, so whole jobs in its answer would carry every Output; a listed job carries how the job stands without its content, so the answer does not grow with the Outputs. Reading a job takes `job:read`, so a key holding only `job:create` learns from these answers how each job stands but not its Output, failure message or activity. The hub reads the answered jobs with the list's projection, so Postgres does not read those columns, and reads them with the Batch in one statement (ADR 0048).

The two limits bound different things. The size bounds what the hub reads whole and parses before it handles a request. The count bounds what the hub does for one request: the job rows one transaction inserts, and the answer, a job for each. Bytes of request bound neither, since a job that names its Input files by digest is small in the request. The rows of those files are bounded by the size alone, as a job may name many. A person meets both limits, and each takes one plain sentence where they submit: a number of jobs and a size.

The count was 100 before, with no reason recorded (elflodge/elflodge#33). Then a job's Input files travelled inline in the request and the hub set no limit on a request's size, so the count was a request's only bound. Input files are now uploaded before the jobs that name them (ADR 0035), and a request's body holds at most 16 MiB, so bytes bound the request and the count is left to bound the hub's work for it.

Measured on a hub run from source against Postgres 18 on loopback, a request of 1,000 jobs with 1 KiB of text each, 1.06 MB answered with 440 KB, took 0.22 s on Node 26, the hub's memory grew by 9 MiB, and other reads made meanwhile were answered in under 0.1 s; on a local workerd it took 0.3 to 0.6 s. An exact repeat, which creates nothing, took 0.14 to 0.18 s on Node and 0.13 to 0.24 s on workerd. With each of the 1,000 jobs naming a distinct uploaded file, it took 0.23 s on Node and 0.3 to 0.6 s on workerd. 1,000 jobs in a body one byte under 16 MiB took 0.33 s on Node (median of 5); beside a second create of 1,000 jobs of 1 KiB, it took 0.52 s and the other 0.35 s on Node, the hub's memory going from 231 to 323 MiB, and 0.91 and 0.56 s on workerd, from 270 to 443 MiB. Nothing in the hub or its SQL depends on the count: job and file rows enter their statements as one JSON parameter, and lookups bind arrays, so a statement's parameters do not grow with the jobs. These figures support 1,000 and say nothing about more.

Answered as listed, a repeat naming 1,000 ended jobs answered 631 KB whether their Outputs held 2 KiB, 50 KiB or 500 KiB each, by create and by add, in 0.10 to 0.21 s on Node and 0.16 to 0.22 s on workerd, the range of the medians of three trials of each case. 100 such jobs answered 63 KB, and a read of one of them still gave its 500 KiB Output.

## Considered options

- Keep 100, and send a larger Batch in groups: a stop between groups, from a lost connection or a refusal in a later group, leaves a Batch that exists with part of its jobs and is already running them.
- No count, only the 16 MiB: short jobs, or jobs naming their files, would let one request carry hundreds of thousands of jobs, each a row inserted in one transaction and a job in the answer, at sizes nobody measured; and a limit stated only in bytes does not tell a person how many jobs fit.
- A draft Batch sealed across requests: ADR 0049 rules it out until a caller needs atomic submission across requests. A Batch beyond these limits still goes in groups, each committed on its own.
- Answer whole jobs: a repeat naming 1,000 ended jobs with Outputs of 50 KiB each answered 51.8 MB, in 0.8 s on Node and 1.3 s on workerd; with 500 KiB each, 512.6 MB in 9 s, the hub's memory peaking at 2,779 MiB on Node and 3,694 MiB on workerd; and 100 such jobs, the earlier limit, already answered 51.3 MB.
- Answer the whole job with only its Output left out: a third form of a job to keep beside the job and the listed job, and a repeat would still carry up to 1,000 failure messages of up to 4,096 characters each, and the activity of its running jobs.
- Answer each job's `id` and `customId` alone: a caller who lost the answer could not see how each held job stands without reading each one, and `jobs submit`, which prints the job it submitted, would need `job:read` beside `job:create`.

## Consequences

An add holds the Batch's lock (ADR 0033) while it inserts. Adding 1,000 jobs to a Batch of 200 with 20 jobs running, each reporting every 100 ms, with the breaker on, took 0.18 to 0.22 s on Node and 0.25 to 0.43 s on workerd (medians of 3). Behind it, with no add, an add of 100 and an add of 1,000, a job's end was acknowledged in 58, 62 and 150 ms on Node and 123, 101 and 248 ms on workerd, and a pause in 75, 71 and 152 ms on Node and 130, 139 and 228 ms on workerd: each waited about 0.1 s longer behind an add of 1,000. A second add waits for the whole of the first: an add of 100 jobs was answered in 122, 82 and 174 ms on Node and 115, 200 and 431 ms on workerd. Progress reports do not take the Batch's lock and did not wait.

On Node 26, one request at a time (medians of 5), a create of 1,000 jobs of 1 KiB of text used 135 ms of CPU, its heap going from 54 to 85 MiB; 1,000 jobs each naming its own uploaded file 103 ms; 1,000 jobs in a body one byte under 16 MiB 297 ms, its heap going from 49 to 104 MiB with 75 MiB outside the heap; and an add of 1,000 jobs 101 ms.

On Cloudflare Workers, a request may use 30 s of CPU by default on the paid plan and 10 ms on the free plan, an isolate has 128 MB of memory, a request's body may hold 100 MB, and a Hyperdrive query may take 60 s. The body's 16 MiB is well under 100 MB, and the longest statement measured in these runs took 5.7 s of the 60 s, run against Postgres directly; pooling and timeouts through Hyperdrive were not tested. A local workerd reports neither CPU time nor an isolate's memory, so the CPU figures above are a Node process's, and a hosted hub's CPU and an isolate's memory are unknown until it is deployed. On Node, a create of 1,000 jobs used far less than the paid plan's 30 s, and one of 100 jobs already used 63 ms, more than the free plan's 10 ms. A request at the largest body took memory of the order of 128 MB on Node.

## When to reconsider

Raise the count when a measurement at a larger one, on Node and on Workers, shows the time, memory and answer of a request still small, or when callers need Batches of more than 1,000 jobs to exist whole, which is also when ADR 0049 reconsiders a draft Batch. Lower the count, or the size, if a deployed Workers hub runs out of an isolate's memory on a request at the limits, or if what waits on a Batch's lock comes to wait for its adds. Answer whole jobs again if a byte limit on Outputs and failure messages bounds what a repeat would carry.
