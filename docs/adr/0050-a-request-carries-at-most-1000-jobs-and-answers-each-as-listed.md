# A request carries at most 1,000 jobs and answers each as the job list gives it

`POST /batches` and `POST /batches/{id}/jobs` each take up to 1,000 jobs in a body of at most 16 MiB, and commit them together (ADR 0049). A Batch within both limits is created whole or not at all, through the API and through `batches submit`, which packs jobs into requests by both. A request over either limit is refused and makes nothing: more jobs with 400 pointing at `/jobs`, more bytes with 413. A larger Batch is sent in several requests, each committed on its own.

Both answer each job the request names as `GET /jobs` lists it, beside whether the request created it and the Batch: without its Output, its failure's message or its activity, which a read of the job gives. A request repeated after its jobs ended names every one of them, so a whole job in the answer would carry each one's Output. Answering whole jobs, 1,000 ended jobs with Outputs of 50 KiB each answered 51.8 MB, in 0.8 s on Node and 1.3 s on workerd; with 500 KiB each, 512.6 MB in 9 s, the hub's memory peaking at 2.8 GB on Node and 3.7 GB on workerd; and 100 such jobs, the earlier limit, already answered 51.3 MB. The hub reads the answered jobs with the list's projection, so Postgres does not read those columns, and reads them with the Batch in one statement (ADR 0048).

The two limits bound different things. The size bounds what the hub reads whole and parses before it handles a request. The count bounds what the hub does for one request: the rows one transaction inserts and locks, and the answer, a job for each. Bytes of request bound neither, since a job that names its Input files by digest is small in the request. A person meets both limits, and each takes one plain sentence where they submit: a number of jobs and a size.

The count was 100 before, with no reason recorded (elflodge/elflodge#33). Then a job's Input files travelled inline in the request and the hub set no limit on a request's size, so the count was a request's only bound. Input files are now uploaded before the jobs that name them (ADR 0035), and a request's body holds at most 16 MiB, so bytes bound the request and the count is left to bound the hub's work for it.

Measured on a hub run from source against Postgres 18 on loopback, a request of 1,000 jobs with 1 KiB of text each, 1.06 MB answered with 440 KB, took 0.22 s on Node 26, the hub's memory grew by 9 MiB, and other reads made meanwhile were answered in under 0.1 s; on a local workerd it took 0.3 to 0.6 s. An exact repeat, which creates nothing, took 0.14 to 0.18 s on Node and 0.13 to 0.24 s on workerd. With each of the 1,000 jobs naming a distinct uploaded file, it took 0.23 s on Node and 0.3 to 0.6 s on workerd, and a body one byte under 16 MiB took 0.7 s. Nothing in the hub or its SQL depends on the count: job and file rows enter their statements as one JSON parameter, and lookups bind arrays, so a statement's parameters do not grow with the jobs. These figures support 1,000 and say nothing about more.

## Considered options

- Keep 100, and send a larger Batch in groups: a stop between groups, from a lost connection or a refusal in a later group, leaves a Batch that exists with part of its jobs and is already running them.
- No count, only the 16 MiB: short jobs, or jobs naming their files, would let one request carry hundreds of thousands of jobs, each a row inserted in one transaction and a job in the answer, at sizes nobody measured; and a limit stated only in bytes does not tell a person how many jobs fit.
- A draft Batch sealed across requests: ADR 0049 rules it out until a caller needs atomic submission across requests. A Batch beyond these limits still goes in groups, each committed on its own.
- Answer the whole job with only its Output left out: a third form of a job to keep beside the job and the listed job, and a repeat would still carry up to 1,000 failure messages of 4,096 characters, 4.1 MB, and the activity of its running jobs.
- Answer each job's `id` and `customId` alone: a caller who lost the answer could not see how each held job stands without reading each one, and `jobs submit`, which prints the job it submitted, would need `job:read` beside `job:create`.

## Consequences

An add holds the Batch's lock (ADR 0033) while it inserts. Adding 1,000 jobs to a Batch of 200 with 20 jobs running, each reporting every 100 ms, with the breaker on, took 0.18 to 0.22 s on Node and 0.25 to 0.43 s on workerd (medians of 3). What waits on the Batch's lock waited about 0.1 s longer behind it, bounded by the add: with no add, an add of 100 and an add of 1,000, a job's end was acknowledged in 58, 62 and 150 ms on Node and 123, 101 and 248 ms on workerd; a pause in 75, 71 and 152 ms on Node and 130, 139 and 228 ms on workerd; and a second add of 100 jobs in 122, 82 and 174 ms on Node and 115, 200 and 431 ms on workerd. Progress reports do not take the Batch's lock and did not wait.

Two creates of 1,000 jobs side by side, one of them a body one byte under 16 MiB, took 0.52 and 0.35 s on Node, the hub's memory going from 231 to 323 MiB, and 0.91 and 0.56 s on workerd, from 270 to 443 MiB. On Node 26, one request at a time (medians of 5), a create of 1,000 jobs of 1 KiB of text used 135 ms of CPU, its heap going from 54 to 85 MiB; 1,000 jobs each naming its own uploaded file 103 ms; 1,000 jobs in a body one byte under 16 MiB 297 ms, its heap going from 49 to 104 MiB with 75 MiB outside the heap; and an add of 1,000 jobs 101 ms.

On Cloudflare Workers, a request may use 30 s of CPU by default on the paid plan and 10 ms on the free plan, an isolate has 128 MB of memory, a request's body may hold 100 MB, and a Hyperdrive query may take 60 s. A local workerd reports neither CPU time nor an isolate's memory, so the Node figures are the nearest evidence: the CPU fits the paid plan with a wide margin, while a submission of 100 jobs already used 63 ms, so none of these fits the free plan's 10 ms. Whether a request at the largest body stays within an isolate's 128 MB is not known: on Node it took memory of that order, and only a deployed hub settles it.

## When to reconsider

Raise the count when a measurement at a larger one, on Node and on Workers, shows the time, memory and answer of a request still small, or when callers need Batches of more than 1,000 jobs to exist whole, which is also when ADR 0049 reconsiders a draft Batch. Lower the count, or the size, if a deployed Workers hub runs out of an isolate's memory on a request at the limits, or if what waits on a Batch's lock comes to wait for its adds. Answer whole jobs again if a byte limit on Outputs and failure messages bounds what a repeat would carry.
